## An Interesting Read

[The Rise of Worse is Better](https://www.dreamsongs.com/RiseOfWorseIsBetter.html) by Richard P. Gabriel

This essay explores a counterintuitive idea in software design: that simpler, "good enough" solutions often beat more correct and complete ones in practice. Gabriel contrasts two design philosophies — the "MIT approach," which prioritizes correctness and consistency above all else, and the "New Jersey approach," which favors simplicity of implementation even at the cost of some elegance. The argument is that software built under the "worse is better" philosophy spreads faster because it's easier to port, easier to understand, and easier to get running on new systems.

What makes this piece so compelling is how relevant it still is decades after it was written. You can see the same tension playing out today in debates over microservices vs. monoliths, or in how scrappy startups with minimal viable products routinely outpace larger teams building "the right way." It's a good reminder that shipping something imperfect and iterating on it is often more valuable than chasing perfection from the start — a lesson that applies well beyond just software engineering.

## Additional Reflection (PR)

One thing that stuck with me after reading this again is how "worse is better" connects to the way open source projects grow. The most widely adopted tools aren't always the most technically impressive — they're the ones that are easy to pick up and contribute to. A project with a lower barrier to entry attracts more contributors, which leads to more improvements over time. In that sense, the "worse" starting point actually creates a better long-term outcome through community momentum.

It also made me think about how this applies to learning. As a student, it's tempting to want to fully understand something before building anything, but in practice you learn faster by jumping in and making mistakes. Getting a rough version working and iterating on it teaches you more than spending weeks planning the perfect solution. That's essentially the same tradeoff Gabriel describes — favoring progress over perfection.
